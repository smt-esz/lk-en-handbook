    Spoken text types

    Speaking is assessed in the final examination, in class presentations and in every discussion you take part in. This is the quick lookup for everything you say.

    At a glance
    Purpose Find the conventions of a spoken text type a task asks you to produce
    Structure One block per text type, always the same five lines
    Register & voice Every block names both, because markers check both
    Length Where a task gives no word count, use the figures here as a default
    Watch out The task always beats this page. If it names a register or an addressee, that wins

    The five lines below mean the same thing on every page of this handbook:

    • Purpose — what the text is supposed to do to its reader or listener
    • Structure — the skeleton a marker expects to find
    • Register & voice — formality, grammatical person, tense
    • Length — a workable default when the task is silent
    • Watch out — the mistake that costs most marks in this particular type

    Presentation
    Purpose Make an audience understand and remember something they did not know
    Structure Greeting → attention-grabbing introduction → main part → conclusion → invitation to questions
    Register & voice Neutral to formal. First person, addressing the audience directly. Simple present
    Length As set. Roughly 130 words per minute of speech
    Watch out Do not read full sentences from your notes. Explain technical terms, avoid words your classmates will not know, use visuals to support rather than repeat you. In a group presentation every member must carry a real share

    Speech
    Purpose Move an audience to think, feel or do something
    Structure Introduction → main part → conclusion, all serving one message
    Register & voice Neutral to formal. First person, direct address. Simple present
    Length As set. Roughly 130 words per minute
    Watch out State your intention clearly. Pauses, emphasis and eye contact are part of the performance and are marked. Full page: How to write a speech

    Discussion
    Purpose Work through an issue with others and let positions meet
    Structure Open with your position → argue with examples → respond to others → move towards a shared conclusion
    Register & voice Neutral to formal. First person. Simple present
    Length As set
    Watch out Participate without dominating. Listen, refer back to what others said, take turns. This is the same skill the paired speaking exam tests: The speaking exam

    Dialogue
    Purpose Play out an exchange between two people convincingly
    Structure Opening → exchange with genuine turn-taking → closing
    Register & voice Set by the roles. First person in role. Tense as the situation requires
    Length As set
    Watch out Stay in role, react to what your partner actually said, and keep it polite even when the roles disagree

    Interview
    Purpose Draw information or a position out of someone for an audience
    Structure Introduction of guest and topic → questions grouped sensibly → closing thanks
    Register & voice Neutral to formal. First and second person. Simple present, past for what happened
    Length As set
    Watch out Open questions produce answers, closed questions produce nothing. Follow up on what the guest says instead of working through a fixed list

    Podcast
    Purpose Hold a listener who cannot see you and cannot skim
    Structure Title and topic → introduction that hooks → main part → conclusion
    Register & voice Informal to neutral, matched to the audience. Neutral or first person. Simple present
    Length As set
    Watch out Signpost far more than you would in writing. Imagery, exaggeration and rhetorical questions carry a listener; music, quotations or a short interview clip can support a point

    Role play
    Purpose Act out a situation from a defined perspective
    Structure As the scenario requires
    Register & voice Determined by your role, not by your own habits
    Length As set
    Watch out Everything you say should be something your character would say. Breaking role to comment on it costs marks

    The pattern behind all of them

    Spoken types are judged on the same five questions as written ones, with one addition that never appears in writing: delivery. Volume, pace, eye contact, gesture and the use of pauses are marked, not decoration.

    • What is this supposed to do to the people listening?
    • What skeleton will they expect?
    • What register, what person, what tense?
    • How long, and does the task say?
    • What would make this fail its type?
    • Can I be heard, followed and looked at while I do it?
