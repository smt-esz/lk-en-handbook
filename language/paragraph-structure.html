    Paragraph structure and cohesion

    Both "topic sentences" and "cohesion" are named directly in the marking scheme, see Exams & grading.

    At a glance
    Purpose Make a paragraph's job visible in its first sentence, and make paragraphs read as one connected argument, not a pile of separate ones
    Structure Topic sentence → supporting sentences → (optional) linking sentence into the next paragraph
    Register & voice Applies to every formal text type in this handbook equally
    Length A topic sentence is one sentence, not a heading and not a whole paragraph
    Watch out A topic sentence that only names a topic, without making a claim about it, does not count as one

    What a topic sentence actually is

    A topic sentence does two things at once: it names what the paragraph is about, and it makes a claim about it. Naming the topic alone is not enough.

    • Not a topic sentence: This paragraph is about social media.
    • Topic sentence: Social media has changed how teenagers form friendships, for better and for worse.

    Everything else in the paragraph exists to support that one claim, developed with evidence and explanation, not a list of loosely related points.

    Where it goes

    Almost always the first sentence of the paragraph. Readers, and markers, use it to predict what is coming; saving it for the middle or the end makes a paragraph harder to follow even when the content itself is good. Creative writing is the exception, where withholding information can be the point, but never in analysis, comment, or discussion paragraphs.

    Supporting sentences

    Every sentence after the topic sentence should develop the claim it makes: evidence, an example, an explanation, a consequence. A sentence that does not connect back to the topic sentence belongs in a different paragraph, or nowhere.

    Linking to the next paragraph

    A short closing or transition sentence can point forward to the next paragraph's topic, useful in longer essays such as a discussion essay. This is optional, and it is not a conclusion: a conclusion belongs at the end of the whole text, not at the end of every paragraph.

    Cohesion: connecting the whole text

    Cohesion is what the marking scheme means by "connectives, referring back, repeating words and structures, paragraphing" under text organization. It works above the level of a single paragraph.

    • Connectives signal how ideas relate: addition (furthermore, in addition), contrast (however, on the other hand), cause and effect (as a result, consequently), sequence (first, finally). Vary them, repeating also or but every time flattens the argument.
    • Referring back ties a sentence to something already mentioned, using pronouns (it, this, these) or a summarizing noun (this approach, such claims), instead of repeating the full phrase each time.
    • Repeating words and structures on purpose can bind sentences together, a key term echoed across a paragraph, or a deliberately parallel sentence structure. The same connective opening every paragraph is the opposite: it makes the structure feel mechanical rather than connected.
    • Paragraphing is itself a form of cohesion: one paragraph, one idea. A wall of text with no breaks reads as unstructured even when every sentence individually makes sense.

    Common mistakes

    • A "topic sentence" that only announces the topic, with no claim: This paragraph will discuss climate change.
    • Starting every paragraph with the same connective
    • A paragraph that drifts away from its own topic sentence halfway through
    • One long paragraph covering the whole argument, with no breaks at all
