# Discussions and debates

Two different formats with two different goals. A discussion looks for the best answer together. A debate assigns you a side and asks you to win. Both are marked on how you deal with other people, not only on what you know.

!!! tip "At a glance"

    |   |   |
    |---|---|
    | **Purpose** | Discussion: work out an issue together. Debate: argue an assigned side under fixed rules |
    | **Structure** | Discussion: position → argument → response → shared conclusion. Debate: opening speeches → rebuttals → closing speeches |
    | **Register & voice** | Neutral to formal. First person. Simple present. Polite even when you disagree sharply |
    | **Length** | As set. In a debate the speaking times are part of the rules |
    | **Watch out** | **Participate without dominating.** Listening and responding are marked as heavily as speaking |

**Operators that show up in this task type:**

| Operator | Example task |
|---|---|
| **discuss** (AFB III) | *Discuss in pairs whether social media should be regulated more strictly.* |
| **justify** (AFB III) | *Justify your position after listening to your partner's arguments.* |
| **assess** (AFB III) | *Assess the strongest argument you heard from the other side.* |

## Discussion or debate?

|   | Discussion | Debate |
|---|---|---|
| **Goal** | Understand the issue better | Convince the audience or the judges |
| **Your position** | Your own | Assigned, often the one you disagree with |
| **Rules** | Loose, turn-taking by courtesy | Fixed speaking order and times |
| **Success looks like** | The group has moved somewhere | Your side stood up to attack |
| **The real skill** | Building on other people | Anticipating the counterargument |

## How a discussion works

1. **Open with a position, not a hedge.** "It depends" is where you finish, not where you start.
2. **Give one argument with one piece of evidence.** Then stop and let someone answer.
3. **Listen for what is actually said**, not for the gap where you can insert your next point.
4. **Take up what others said by name.** "Building on what Jonas said about …" costs you two seconds and earns interaction marks.
5. **Disagree with the argument, never with the person.**
6. **Ask when you do not follow.** A clarifying question is a contribution, not an admission of weakness.
7. **Move towards a conclusion.** In the last minutes, name where the group agrees and where it does not.

## How a debate works

A common classroom format, adapt to whatever rules you are given:

| Stage | Who | Time |
|---|---|---|
| Opening statement | Proposition | 2 min |
| Opening statement | Opposition | 2 min |
| Free debate | Both sides alternating | 6 min |
| Closing statement | Opposition | 1 min |
| Closing statement | Proposition | 1 min |

Three things decide a debate more often than knowledge does:

- **Definitions.** Whoever defines the terms of the motion has already shaped the ground. Do it in the first thirty seconds.
- **Rebuttal.** A point left unanswered counts as conceded. Answer the strongest argument against you, not the weakest.
- **The closing.** Do not summarize everything. Name the one clash your side won.

## Useful phrases

| Purpose | Phrases |
|---|---|
| Stating a position | <ul><li>My position is that …</li><li>I would argue that …</li><li>The way I see it, …</li><li>We on this side believe that …</li></ul> |
| Supporting it | <ul><li>The reason is straightforward: …</li><li>Take the example of …</li><li>The figures back this up: …</li></ul> |
| Building on someone | <ul><li>Building on what … said, …</li><li>I would like to pick up on that point.</li><li>That is exactly why I think …</li><li>I agree, and I would go one step further: …</li></ul> |
| Disagreeing politely | <ul><li>I see it differently, because …</li><li>I take your point, but consider …</li><li>I am afraid I cannot agree with that.</li><li>That may be true in general, but here …</li></ul> |
| Rebutting in a debate | <ul><li>My opponent claims that … . That does not hold, because …</li><li>The other side has not answered the question of …</li><li>Even if we accept that …, it does not follow that …</li></ul> |
| Asking for clarification | <ul><li>Sorry, do you mean that … ?</li><li>Could you say more about … ?</li><li>Can you give an example of what you mean?</li></ul> |
| Coming in | <ul><li>Could I come in here?</li><li>May I add something to that?</li><li>If I may, …</li></ul> |
| Holding the floor | <ul><li>If I could just finish this point.</li><li>Let me make one more thing clear, then I will hand over.</li></ul> |
| Bringing someone in | <ul><li>What is your take on this, … ?</li><li>Would you go that far?</li><li>We have not heard from … yet.</li></ul> |
| Concluding together | <ul><li>So we seem to agree that …</li><li>Where we differ is …</li><li>The one thing we have not resolved is …</li></ul> |
| Closing a debate | <ul><li>This debate came down to one question: …</li><li>The other side never answered …</li><li>For these reasons, we urge you to support the motion.</li></ul> |

## Buying yourself a second

Everyone needs thinking time. In a foreign language you need slightly more, and silence is more expensive than a filler. Use a full sentence rather than "ehm":

- That is a difficult one. Let me think for a moment.
- I want to get this right, so let me put it another way.
- Two things come to mind. First, …

## Checklist for a discussion or debate

- [ ] Did I state a clear position rather than hedging?
- [ ] Did every argument come with evidence or an example?
- [ ] Did I refer to at least two other speakers by what they said?
- [ ] Did I disagree with an argument without attacking a person?
- [ ] Did I ask at least one genuine question?
- [ ] Did I leave space for people who had not spoken?
- [ ] In a debate: did I answer the strongest point against my side?
- [ ] In a debate: does my closing name the clash rather than summarize everything?

## Common mistakes

- Waiting for a gap to deliver a prepared speech instead of responding to what is happening
- Agreeing with everything, which produces a short flat conversation and costs marks in content and interaction alike
- Dominating. Talking for eight of ten minutes loses the same category as saying nothing
- Attacking the person: "That is a stupid argument" instead of "That argument does not work here, because …"
- Leaving the strongest counterargument unanswered and hoping nobody noticed
- Reading from notes. Notes are keywords, a conversation cannot be scripted
