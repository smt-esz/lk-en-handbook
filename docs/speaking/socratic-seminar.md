# Socratic seminar

A seminar is not a debate and not a normal class discussion. Nobody wins it. The group works its way into a text together, and the questions matter more than the answers.

!!! tip "At a glance"

    |   |   |
    |---|---|
    | **Purpose** | Understand the ideas, issues and values in a text by talking through them as a group |
    | **Structure** | Preparation with the text → opening question → the group carries the conversation → closing reflection |
    | **Register & voice** | Neutral to formal. First person. Tentative rather than assertive |
    | **Length** | Usually one lesson. Inner circle around 20 to 30 minutes |
    | **Watch out** | **You are not here to win.** Asserting opinions or proving an argument is the one thing a seminar is not for |

**Operators that show up in this task type:**

| Operator | Example task |
|---|---|
| **interpret** (AFB III) | *Interpret what the text suggests about the narrator's state of mind.* |
| **discuss** (AFB III) | *Discuss how your readings of the text differ within the group.* |
| **relate** (AFB II) | *Relate this text to the ideas raised in the text discussed last week.* |

See [Operators](../language/operators.md) for what each of these actually asks for.

## What makes it different

|   | Socratic seminar | Debate | Normal discussion |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Goal** | Understand the text together | Convince | Exchange views |
| **Your job** | Ask, listen, connect | Argue a side | State your view |
| **Who leads** | The group | The rules | Usually the teacher |
| **Evidence** | Always from the text | Anything persuasive | Often personal experience |
| **A good moment** | Someone changes their mind | Someone is cornered | Someone makes a point |

The teacher is not the one asking the questions. That takes some getting used to, and the first two minutes are always the hardest. Somebody has to start, and the silence before that is normal.

## Preparing the text

Preparation is the whole seminar. An unprepared circle runs out after four minutes.

1. **Read carefully and take your time.** Once for the sense, once with a pencil.
2. **Note what strikes you.** Significant points, images that recur, sentences you had to read twice.
3. **Read critically.** What is the purpose of this text? What are its central themes? What is it not saying?
4. **Identify the key ideas, arguments and perspectives** it puts forward.
5. **Write questions.** Open, genuinely open. Three is a good target, and see the next section.
6. **Collect evidence.** For every idea you want to raise, mark the line you would point to.
7. **Consider the other reading.** What would someone who disagrees with you say, and where in the text would they look?

## What makes a good seminar question

A question you already know the answer to is not a question, it is a quiz item.

| Weak | Better |
|---|---|
| Who is the narrator? | What does the narrator want us not to notice? |
| Is the lion a good character? | What does the text seem to think we owe to someone who once had power over us? |
| Does the tree love the boy? | Where does giving stop being generous in this text? |
| What happens at the end? | Why does the text end here rather than one page later? |

Three types worth having in your notes:

- **Opening question.** Broad, no single answer, points back to the text. Good for the first five minutes.
- **Core question.** Goes at the heart of the text's problem. This is the one you hope the group spends most of its time on.
- **Closing question.** Connects the text to something beyond it, without leaving it behind.

## Language for the circle

| Purpose | Phrases |
|---|---|
| Asking for clarification | <ul><li>Can you please elaborate on that?</li><li>I am not sure I understand. Can you clarify?</li><li>Can you give me an example of what you mean?</li><li>Can you explain that in a different way?</li><li>I am sorry, I do not quite follow.</li></ul> |
| Checking understanding | <ul><li>What are you assuming when you say … ?</li><li>If I understand correctly, … . Is that right?</li><li>How does this relate to what we have been discussing?</li><li>I want to make sure I understood you correctly. Did you mean … ?</li></ul> |
| Questioning and evaluating | <ul><li>Can you explain why this is important?</li><li>Can you provide some evidence for that?</li><li>How did you come to that conclusion?</li><li>Can you explain your reasoning?</li><li>What are your overall impressions of this passage?</li></ul> |
| Disagreeing | <ul><li>I am afraid I do not agree with that statement.</li><li>I see things differently.</li><li>I understand your point of view, but I disagree, because …</li><li>I disagree with your conclusion, because …</li></ul> |
| Agreeing and adding | <ul><li>I could not agree more.</li><li>I support that.</li><li>I am on the same page.</li><li>I agree and would like to add …</li><li>I completely agree and I would also like to point out …</li></ul> |
| Anchoring in the text | <ul><li>If we look at line 12, …</li><li>The text actually says …, which is not quite the same thing.</li><li>There is a word here that I keep coming back to: …</li></ul> |
| Opening space | <ul><li>We have not heard from everyone yet.</li><li>Does anyone read that differently?</li><li>Could we stay with this passage a little longer?</li></ul> |
| Changing your mind | <ul><li>I came in thinking …, and I am no longer sure.</li><li>That reading is better than mine, because …</li></ul> |

Changing your mind out loud is the most valuable thing that can happen in a seminar. It is not a defeat, and it is worth saying when it happens.

## If you are in the outer circle

Many seminars run with an inner circle talking and an outer circle observing. The outer circle is not a break.

- Follow one speaker and note how often they build on someone else
- Note where the conversation lost the text and started running on opinion
- Note the question that produced the most movement
- Be ready to feed that back afterwards

## Checklist for a Socratic seminar

- [ ] Have I read the text at least twice, with a pencil?
- [ ] Do I have three open questions written down?
- [ ] For each idea I want to raise, do I know the line I would point to?
- [ ] Have I thought about how someone might read this differently?
- [ ] Do I know six phrases for clarifying and disagreeing?
- [ ] Am I ready to speak in the first five minutes?
- [ ] Am I ready to stop talking and let a silence sit?

## Common mistakes

- Arriving unprepared. It is visible within two minutes and it costs the whole group
- Treating it as a debate and trying to win
- Opinion without text. "I just feel that …" with nothing to point at
- Speaking to the teacher instead of to the circle
- Waiting for the perfect sentence. An imperfect contribution in English beats a perfect one you never said
- Filling every silence. A pause usually means people are thinking
