# How to write a characterization

A characterization is an analysis with a person at the centre. Everything you claim about a character has to come from the text, and the interesting part is almost never what a character says about themselves.

!!! tip "At a glance"

    |   |   |
    |---|---|
    | **Purpose** | Show what a character is like and how the text makes us see them that way |
    | **Structure** | Introduction → outward facts → behaviour and relationships → inner life → function in the text |
    | **Register & voice** | Formal, neutral perspective, **simple present** |
    | **Length** | As set by the task |
    | **Watch out** | **Statement → evidence (quote + line) → effect.** A list of adjectives is not a characterization |

**Operators for this task type:** characters are usually set through **analyse**, not through the word "characterize" itself, keep that in mind.

| Operator | Example task |
|---|---|
| **analyse** — most common, remember this one | *Analyse Hector's character and the way Alan Bennett creates it.* (Abitur 2009) |
| **compare** | *Compare Hector's and Irwin's attitudes towards education.* (Abitur 2009, same paper) |
| **characterize** | Not on the official core list, but it names this page and may turn up. Rarely the literal task word |

See [Operators](../language/operators.md#writing).

## Direct and indirect characterization

This distinction carries most of the marks.

| | Where it comes from | How much it is worth |
|---|---|---|
| **Direct** | The narrator or another character tells us what someone is like | Easy to find, easy to be misled by |
| **Indirect** | What the character does, says, thinks, how others react to them, how they are described | Where the real analysis happens |

Direct statements are evidence about the **speaker** as much as about the person described. If a narrator calls someone selfish, the first question is whether the narrator is reliable.

## Step by step

1. **Read with one character in mind.** Second reading, pencil, only their scenes.
2. **Collect in four columns:** what they say, what they do, what they think, how others react to them.
3. **Look for the gap.** Between what a character says and what they do, between how they see themselves and how others see them. That gap is usually the point of the character.
4. **Group your findings into traits.** Three or four well-evidenced traits beat eight assertions.
5. **Order them.** Outward and factual first, inner life last, because the inner life is what you had to work for.
6. **Write each paragraph as claim, evidence, effect.** Trait, quotation with line reference, what it tells us and how the text achieves it.
7. **Ask what the character is for.** Do they drive the plot, embody a theme, act as a foil to someone else? This is the paragraph that lifts a characterization out of the middle band.
8. **Check for development.** Is the character at the end the same as at the beginning? If not, name the turning point.

## Structure in detail

| Section | What goes in it |
|---|---|
| Introduction | Title, author, year, text type, and who this character is in one sentence |
| Outward facts | Age, background, occupation, appearance, social position, only where the text gives them |
| Behaviour and relationships | How they act, how they treat others, how others react to them |
| Language | How they speak: [register](../language/register-and-tone.md), sentence length, what they avoid saying |
| Inner life | Motives, fears, values, contradictions |
| Development | Do they change? Where, and what triggers it? |
| Function | What the character does for the text: theme, foil, contrast, plot |

## Useful phrases

| Purpose | Phrases |
|---|---|
| Introducing the character | <ul><li>… is the first-person narrator of …</li><li>The reader first meets … in the opening paragraph, where …</li><li>… is introduced as a minor figure and turns out to be central to …</li></ul> |
| Direct characterization | <ul><li>The narrator describes her as "…" (l. 12).</li><li>According to …, he is "…" (l. 30), although the text later undercuts this.</li></ul> |
| Indirect characterization | <ul><li>His behaviour towards … reveals that …</li><li>The way she addresses her mother suggests …</li><li>That he says nothing at this point is more telling than any answer.</li><li>Other characters react to him with …, which indicates …</li></ul> |
| Language as evidence | <ul><li>Her short, clipped sentences convey …</li><li>He uses the vocabulary of business even when talking about his family, which …</li><li>The repeated "…" (ll. 5, 19) marks him as …</li></ul> |
| Naming a contradiction | <ul><li>What he says and what he does pull in different directions: …</li><li>She presents herself as …, yet the text shows her …</li></ul> |
| Development | <ul><li>Up to this point, … . After the encounter with …, however, …</li><li>The turning point comes in l. 41, when …</li><li>By the end of the extract, she has moved from … to …</li></ul> |
| Function | <ul><li>… serves as a foil to …, throwing his … into relief.</li><li>Through …, the text explores the question of …</li><li>The character embodies the conflict between … and …</li></ul> |
| Concluding | <ul><li>Taken together, these details present a character who …</li><li>The text builds … less through description than through …</li></ul> |

## Checklist for a characterization

- [ ] Does every trait I name have a quotation with a line reference?
- [ ] Have I used indirect evidence, not only what the narrator states?
- [ ] Have I checked whether the direct statements come from a reliable source?
- [ ] Have I found and named at least one contradiction or gap?
- [ ] Have I used the character's own language as evidence?
- [ ] Have I said what the character is **for** in this text?
- [ ] Have I checked whether the character develops, and named the turning point?
- [ ] Simple present throughout?
- [ ] No more than four consecutive words taken from the source?

## Common mistakes

- A list of adjectives with no evidence: "She is brave, intelligent and stubborn"
- Retelling the plot in the order it happens, with the character mentioned along the way
- Taking the narrator at their word without asking whether they are reliable
- Judging the character morally instead of analysing them
- Missing the function paragraph, which is where the analysis stops being a description
- Slipping into the past tense because the story is told in the past tense
