# How to write a creative text

In the examination this is one of the two options in the final sub-task. The other is a personal response. Both are marked with the same rigour, and creative does not mean free: you are writing inside a text somebody else made.

!!! tip "At a glance"

    |   |   |
    |---|---|
    | **Purpose** | Extend, continue or re-angle a given text without contradicting it |
    | **Structure** | The structure of the form you were asked for: diary entry, letter, monologue, continuation |
    | **Register & voice** | The character's, not yours. Tense follows the form |
    | **Length** | As set by the task, typically 200 to 300 words |
    | **Watch out** | **Nothing may contradict the source.** Invention is welcome, contradiction is a content error |

**Operators for this task type:** creative briefs skip the analytical operators, the task simply says **write**, and the brief itself sets what the text has to do.

| Instruction | Example task |
|---|---|
| **write** (the only operator used) — remember this one | *Write a love letter to the United Kingdom expressing appreciation of two of the following aspects: history, politics, science, culture.* (Abitur 2017) |
| continue *(not a formal operator, but common in class)* | *Continue the story from the point where the extract breaks off.* |

See [Operators](../language/operators.md) for how the formal operators work in the other task types.

## The rule that decides everything

A creative task is marked on **how well your text fits the one you were given**. The marking criteria ask for convincing ideas realized in the right text type, with a clear sense of situation and addressee, and for reference back to the material.

That means two things at once:

- You **may** invent. Names, weather, a memory, a room, a smell, what happened the next morning.
- You **may not** contradict. Not the events, not the relationships, not what a character can plausibly know at that point in the story.

Every sentence you write should survive the question: *could this be true in the world of the original text?*

## Step by step

1. **Read the task twice and underline three things:** the form, the character or voice, and the moment in the text you are writing from.
2. **Go back to the source and mine it.** What does this character notice? What words do they use? What do they avoid saying? Mark five details you can reuse.
3. **Fix the moment.** Exactly when does your text happen? Before the extract, during it, an hour after, ten years later? Everything else follows from that.
4. **Fix what the character knows at that moment.** Write it down. This is the single most common place marks are lost.
5. **Plan three beats.** Even 250 words need a shape: a situation, a turn, a landing.
6. **Write in their voice, not yours.** Educated adult narrator and frightened fourteen-year-old do not share a vocabulary.
7. **Anchor it in the source at least twice.** Not with quotations, with details a reader of the original will recognize.
8. **Revise for range.** Creative writing is still marked on lexis and grammar. Imagery is not an excuse for loose sentences.

## The four forms you are most likely to get

### Diary entry

|   |   |
|---|---|
| **Voice** | First person, past for the events, present for reflection |
| **Structure** | Date → what happened → what it did to me → an open ending or a resolution |
| **Typical move** | Address the diary directly, use short and sometimes unfinished sentences |
| **Watch out** | Only what the writer could know. No narrator knowledge |

### Interior monologue

|   |   |
|---|---|
| **Voice** | First person, present tense, unfiltered |
| **Structure** | No structure on the surface, a clear one underneath: a thought that circles and shifts |
| **Typical move** | Associative jumps, repetitions, questions with no answer, sentence fragments |
| **Watch out** | It must still be readable. Stream of consciousness is not an excuse for chaos |

### Letter to a character

|   |   |
|---|---|
| **Voice** | First person, addressed to a named person you know something about |
| **Structure** | Salutation → why I am writing → what I want to say → what I want from you → close |
| **Typical move** | Refer to a shared moment from the text so the reader knows you were there |
| **Watch out** | [Register](../language/register-and-tone.md) follows the relationship. A letter to a parent and to a stranger are not the same text |

### Continuation of a story

|   |   |
|---|---|
| **Voice** | The same narrative perspective as the original, unless the task says otherwise |
| **Structure** | Pick up mid-motion, develop, land somewhere |
| **Typical move** | Keep the original's tense, rhythm and level of detail |
| **Watch out** | Do not resolve everything. A continuation that ties up every thread reads as a summary |

## Useful phrases

| Purpose | Phrases |
|---|---|
| Opening a diary entry | <ul><li>I still do not know what to make of today.</li><li>It is late, and I cannot stop thinking about …</li><li>Something happened this afternoon that I need to write down.</li></ul> |
| Interior monologue | <ul><li>Why did I say that. Why did I say that.</li><li>Keep walking. Do not look back.</li><li>Later. I will think about it later.</li></ul> |
| Opening a letter | <ul><li>You will be surprised to hear from me.</li><li>I have started this letter four times.</li><li>There is something I should have said to you that evening.</li></ul> |
| Referring back to the source | <ul><li>Ever since that afternoon at …</li><li>You said something then that I have never forgotten.</li><li>It was the same room, only quieter.</li></ul> |
| Showing feeling without naming it | <ul><li>My hands would not keep still.</li><li>I read the message three times and put the phone face down.</li><li>The bus came. I let it go.</li></ul> |
| Landing an ending | <ul><li>Tomorrow I will tell her. Probably.</li><li>Nothing had changed, and everything had.</li><li>I closed the door quietly, which is not how I usually close doors.</li></ul> |

## Show it, do not label it

The quickest way to lift a creative text out of the middle band is to stop naming feelings.

| Instead of | Try |
|---|---|
| I was very angry | <ul class="cols"><li>I heard my own voice go flat</li><li>I folded the letter along its old creases, again and again</li></ul> |
| She was nervous | <ul class="cols"><li>She checked the time twice in a minute</li><li>Her tea went cold beside her</li></ul> |
| It was a sad moment | <ul class="cols"><li>Nobody moved to turn the light on</li><li>He kept talking about the weather</li></ul> |

## Checklist for a creative text

- [ ] Am I writing the form the task asked for?
- [ ] Is the moment fixed: exactly when does this happen?
- [ ] Does everything my character says fit what they could know then?
- [ ] Does anything I invented contradict the source?
- [ ] Have I anchored the text in the original at least twice?
- [ ] Is the voice the character's rather than mine?
- [ ] Have I shown at least two feelings instead of naming them?
- [ ] Does it have three beats: situation, turn, landing?
- [ ] Have I revised for range as well as for correctness?

## Common mistakes

- Writing a summary of the extract with "Dear diary" in front of it
- Letting the character know something they only learn later in the text, or something only the narrator knows
- Naming feelings instead of showing them: sad, angry, happy, nervous
- Dropping the register: a nineteenth-century character using contemporary slang
- Treating creative as a licence for careless English. Lexis and grammar are marked here exactly as they are in an analysis
- Choosing this option in the examination because it looks shorter. It is not
