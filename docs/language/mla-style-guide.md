# Quoting and citing: a mini MLA style guide

One consistent way to quote, cite and format titles, used the same way on every page of this handbook and expected the same way in every essay you write.

!!! tip "At a glance"

    |   |   |
    |---|---|
    | **Purpose** | Quote and cite evidence so it looks the same in every essay, exam or not |
    | **Structure** | Short quotes inside the sentence, long quotes set off, titles in italics or quotation marks depending on length |
    | **Register & voice** | Formal, consistent punctuation, no decorative quotation marks |
    | **Length** | A quote proves a point, it does not replace your point |
    | **Watch out** | An unintroduced or unexplained quote earns nothing. "Quote-dumping" is one of the most common, most expensive habits |

## Titles: italics or quotation marks

- **Italics** for longer, self-contained works: novels, plays, films, collections. *Pride and Prejudice*, *Death of a Salesman*.
- **Quotation marks** for shorter works or parts of a larger whole: poems, short stories, articles, individual episodes. "The Road Not Taken", "A Modest Proposal".

In handwriting, underline instead of italicizing; the rule is the same.

## Short quotations

Up to about two lines: keep them inside your own sentence, in quotation marks, grammatically integrated. Do not let a quotation stand as its own sentence with no lead-in.

- Weak: *"The wallpaper is yellow." This shows Gilman uses colour symbolically.*
- Better: *Gilman uses colour symbolically, describing the wallpaper as "a smouldering unclean yellow" (l. 47).*

## Long quotations

Four lines or more: set off from your own sentence, on its own indented block, without quotation marks. In an exam essay this is rarely worth it, a shorter quote with more analysis around it almost always earns more than a long block with little comment.

## Citing the source

The whole essay usually refers to one given text, so name it once in full in your introduction (title, author, text type) and then cite briefly after each quote:

- Poems and plays: line numbers, e.g. `(l. 14)`, or act/scene/line if given, e.g. `(II.2.14)`
- Prose extracts: paragraph or page numbers if the task gives them, e.g. `(par. 3)`
- No page numbers given: name the quote's position instead, e.g. *towards the end of the extract*

## Works Cited: listing your sources

The section above covers a class exam, where you quote the one text you were given. As soon as you use **more than one source**, in a presentation, a project, or a longer piece of coursework, list every source you actually cite on its own page at the end, headed **Works Cited**. Each entry is built from the same blocks, in the same order, and you simply skip the blocks that do not apply to your source:

1. Author.
2. Title of source.
3. Title of container,
4. Other contributors,
5. Version,
6. Number,
7. Publisher,
8. Publication date,
9. Location.

**Author.** Last name first, then first name, ended with a period: *Bhabha, Homi K.* Two authors: only the first name is inverted, joined by *and*: *Smith, John, and Ana Weber.* Leave out titles like Dr. or Prof.

**Title of source.** Italics for a whole, standalone work (a book, a website, a film). Quotation marks for something that is part of a larger whole (an article, a poem, a single web page, an episode).

**Title of container.** The larger whole the source sits inside, in italics: the website a page belongs to, the newspaper an article appeared in, the anthology a poem was printed in.

**Location.** Page numbers for print, or the URL for a web source with `http://` removed, ending the entry with a period.

Everything else (a translator, an edition number, a publisher, a date) only gets added when it is actually known and relevant. Most school-level sources need no more than author, title, container and location.

**Examples:**

- Book: *Bhabha, Homi K.* The Location of Culture. *Routledge, 1994.*
- Web article: *Lundman, Susan.* "How to Make Vegetarian Chili." *eHow,* www.ehow.com/how_10727_make-vegetarian-chili.html.
- Newspaper article: *Bagchi, Alaknanda.* "Conflicting Nationalisms." *Tulsa Studies in Women's Literature,* vol. 15, no. 1, 1996, pp. 41-50.

**Formatting the page:**

- Centre the heading **Works Cited**, do not italicize it or put it in quotation marks. The entries themselves stay flush with the left margin.
- Sort entries alphabetically by the author's last name. No known author: alphabetize by the title instead.
- Give the second and any further line of an entry a hanging indent, so the author's name is the only thing that starts at the margin.

## Changing a quotation

- **Omitting words:** mark the gap with an ellipsis in square brackets, `[…]`
- **Adding or changing a word** to fit your sentence grammatically: put it in square brackets, e.g. *the narrator feels "trapped [in her] own home"*
- Never change a quotation silently. If it does not fit your sentence as it stands, quote less or rephrase around it.

## Punctuation with quotation marks

British convention, used throughout this handbook: the full stop goes **outside** the closing quotation mark unless it is part of what you are quoting. See [False friends and German habits](false-friends.md#punctuation-briefly) for the fuller rule on commas and relative clauses.
