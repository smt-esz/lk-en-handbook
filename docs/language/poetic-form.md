# Poetic form and structure

These are the terms for how a poem is built, not what it says. Use them together with [Stylistic devices](stylistic-devices.md), which covers imagery, sound and rhetorical patterns, and with [How to analyse a poem](../analysis/poems.md), which shows how to connect a term like this to an actual argument.

!!! tip "At a glance"

    |   |   |
    |---|---|
    | **Purpose** | Name the building blocks of a poem's form: stanza, rhyme, rhythm, line |
    | **Structure** | Grouped by stanza, rhyme, rhythm and metre, line and syntax, the turn |
    | **Register & voice** | Use these terms the same way you use any analytical vocabulary: formal, simple present |
    | **Length** | Two or three well-explained terms beat a list of ten |
    | **Watch out** | **A term is only worth marks once you connect it to meaning.** Naming a quatrain earns nothing on its own |

## Stanza

A stanza is a poem's paragraph, a group of lines set off from the next group by a space. Stanzas are named by how many lines they have.

| Term | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Stanza | A group of lines set off by a space | — |
| Couplet | Two-line stanza, often rhymed | the final two lines of a Shakespearean sonnet |
| Tercet | Three-line stanza | — |
| Quatrain | Four-line stanza, the most common in English poetry | most hymn and ballad forms |
| Quintain (cinquain) | Five-line stanza | each stanza of Frost's "The Road Not Taken" |
| Sestet | Six-line stanza, also the second half of a sonnet | — |
| Octave | Eight-line stanza, also the first half of a sonnet | — |
| Sonnet | A 14-line poem, usually a single stanza, with a fixed rhyme scheme and a turn | Shakespeare's sonnets |
| Free verse | No fixed rhyme scheme or metre | most 20th- and 21st-century poetry |

## Rhyme

| Term | What it means |
|---|---|
| Rhyme scheme | The pattern of end rhymes, written as letters. The first end sound gets *a*, the next new end sound gets *b*, and so on. A sound that returns reuses its letter |
| End rhyme | Rhyme at the end of lines, the usual kind |
| Internal rhyme | Rhyme within a single line, not just at the end |
| Half rhyme / slant rhyme | Sounds are close but not identical, for example *worth / north* |
| Eye rhyme | Words look like they should rhyme, same spelling pattern, but don't sound alike, for example *love / move* |

To find a rhyme scheme, mark the last word of every line and give matching end sounds the same letter as you go. Write it in the margin, one letter per line, then read the pattern down the stanza.

## Rhythm and metre

You don't need a full scansion of every poem, but knowing the basic units lets you say something precise instead of "the poem has a nice rhythm."

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Metre | A poem's regular pattern of stressed and unstressed syllables |
| Foot | The repeating unit a metre is built from, usually one stressed plus one or more unstressed syllables |
| Iamb / iambic | A foot going unstressed–stressed (da-**DUM**), the most common foot in English |
| Iambic pentameter | Five iambic feet per line, ten syllables, the standard line of the English sonnet and of blank verse |
| Iambic tetrameter | Four iambic feet per line, eight syllables |
| Stressed / unstressed syllable | The syllable that carries more or less emphasis when the line is spoken naturally |

A metre that stays regular throughout a poem is worth noticing. A metre that breaks down at one specific point is usually worth more, because the form is doing something at exactly the place where the content changes too.

## Line and syntax

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Enjambment | A sentence or phrase runs past the end of the line, without a pause, into the next line |
| End-stopped line | The line ends where the sentence or clause ends, with a natural pause |
| Caesura | A pause inside a line, often marked by punctuation such as a comma or dash |

## The turn

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Volta / turn | The point where a poem's argument, mood or perspective shifts. In a sonnet it traditionally sits around line 9, in other poems it can happen anywhere, but it is almost always there somewhere |

Finding the turn is usually the fastest way into what a poem is actually doing, see step 5 in [How to analyse a poem](../analysis/poems.md).

## Common mistakes

- Calling every stanza a quatrain out of habit, without counting the lines
- Writing out a rhyme scheme without checking each sound against the earlier ones, so the letters no longer match real repeats
- Forcing a poem into a perfectly regular metre when it is actually irregular, and missing the one line where it isn't, which is often the point
- Labelling a line break "enjambment" whenever a stanza ends, even where the sentence is already finished
