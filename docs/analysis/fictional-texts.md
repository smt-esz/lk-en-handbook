# How to analyse fictional texts

!!! tip "At a glance"

    |   |   |
    |---|---|
    | **Purpose** | Show **how** a literary text is made and what that does to the reader. Not what happens |
    | **Structure** | Introduction (title, author, year, text type, situation) → analysis along the aspects the task names → conclusion |
    | **Register & voice** | Formal, neutral perspective, **simple present** even for events told in the past tense |
    | **Length** | As set by the task |
    | **Watch out** | Retelling the plot is not analysis. Every claim needs **statement → evidence (quote + line) → effect** |

**Operators for this task type:** Part A1 always pairs an AFB I operator with **analyse**, both are worth remembering.

| Operator | Example task |
|---|---|
| **sum up / outline** (AFB I) | *Sum up what you learn about the protagonist.* (Abitur 2007) |
| **analyse** (AFB II) — appears in every fiction task, remember this one | *Analyze how the author conveys Dell's attitude towards his teenage years. Refer to narrative perspective and language.* (Abitur 2020, spelled as in the original) |
| **compare** (AFB II) | *Compare Hector's and Irwin's attitudes towards education.* (Abitur 2009) |

See [Operators](../language/operators.md).

## Step by step

1. **Read the task first, then the text twice.** First reading for the story, second reading with a pencil: who tells this, who are the characters, where does the mood shift, which words stand out.
2. **Write the introduction.** Title, author, year and text type, then the situation the extract shows, in one or two sentences. No plot summary unless the task asks for one.
3. **Determine the narrative perspective.** This decides how much the reader is allowed to know and whom the reader sympathizes with. See [Narrative perspective](#narrative-perspective) below.
4. **Analyse the characters.** What are they like, how do you know, and how does the author make you know it? See [Characters](#characters).
5. **Analyse setting and atmosphere.** Place, time, social milieu, weather, objects. Setting is rarely decoration; it usually mirrors or contradicts what the characters feel.
6. **Analyse the structure.** Where does the extract begin, where does it turn, how does it end? Sudden beginning, flashback, foreshadowing, open ending?
7. **Analyse the language and imagery.** Word fields, [tone](../language/register-and-tone.md), sentence length, recurring images, symbols, [stylistic devices](../language/stylistic-devices.md). Quote, give the line, explain the effect in context.
8. **Move to theme and intention.** What idea does the text explore, and what attitude does it take towards it? This is where your single observations come together.
9. **Conclude.** Pull the threads together: how do perspective, characters, setting and language produce the effect you described? If the task asks for evaluation or a personal response, keep it separate from the analysis and argue it.

## Narrative perspective

| Perspective | What it does | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| First-person narrator (*I*) | Immediate, subjective, limited to one mind. Invites sympathy | Is the narrator reliable? Does the text hint that we should doubt them? |
| Third-person limited (figural) | Told from outside, but filtered through one character's mind | Whose eyes are we in? What is deliberately withheld? |
| Omniscient (authorial) | Knows everything, can comment, judge, jump in time and place | Does the narrator evaluate the characters, and how? |
| Detached observer | Reports only what could be seen or heard, no inner life | Effect is often cool, clinical, unsettling |

Useful terms:

- **unreliable narrator**: the reader sees more than the narrator admits
- **free indirect speech**: a character's thoughts in the narrator's words, no quotation marks
- **stream of consciousness**: thought reproduced as it comes, often without normal syntax
- **interior monologue**

Ask yourself: what would be lost if this text had a different narrator? That question usually produces your best sentence.

## Characters

**Direct characterization** means the text states a quality: *she was stubborn*. **Indirect characterization** means you infer it from what the character does, says, thinks, from how they speak, and from how other characters react to them. Indirect evidence is the more interesting kind, so look for it.

Terms worth having ready:

- **round** (complex, contradictory) and **flat** (one or two traits)
- **dynamic** (changes in the course of the text) and **static**
- **protagonist**, **antagonist**, **foil** (a character whose contrast makes another one visible)
- **character constellation**: who is close to whom, who is isolated, who is silent

A characterization is not a list of adjectives. Each trait needs a quote and a line reference, and the traits should add up to a picture that has some tension in it.

## Setting and atmosphere

Note place, time, season, weather, light, sounds, objects and social milieu. Then ask what these do:

- Does the setting **mirror** the characters' state, or **contradict** it? Both are meaningful
- Is a place **open or confining**, familiar or strange, safe or threatening?
- Do details recur? A repeated object or image is usually a **motif** and may be a **symbol**

## Text type differences

=== "Prose: short story, novel extract"

    - Short stories often start **in medias res**, leave much unsaid, and end **open**. The gaps are the point; describe what the text withholds and why that works
    - Watch the **turning point** and the last paragraph. Endings carry the meaning
    - Look at the handling of time: is a scene stretched out, are years passed over in a sentence? Slow narration marks importance
    - Typical questions: whose perspective, what changes, what does the ending leave the reader with

=== "Drama extract"

    - Analyse **dialogue as action**: who steers the conversation, who interrupts, who is silent, who talks past the other
    - **Stage directions** are text too. They tell you about tempo, distance, gesture and what a character does while saying something else
    - Look for **subtext**: the gap between what is said and what is meant. Naming this gap is usually the core of a drama analysis
    - Terms: soliloquy, monologue, aside, dramatic irony (the audience knows more than the character), exposition, dramatic climax
    - Note the **constellation on stage**: who enters, who leaves, who is left alone

=== "Poem"

    - Start with the **speaker**: who is speaking, to whom, in what situation and mood? The speaker is not the poet
    - **Form**: number of stanzas and lines, rhyme scheme (write it as abab, aabb …), metre if it is clearly there, line length. Then say what the form does. A regular form can create calm or a cage; a broken form can create unrest
    - **Enjambment** and punctuation control the reading tempo and can push a word into an emphatic position
    - **Sound**: alliteration, assonance, harsh or soft consonants
    - **Imagery** carries most of the meaning in a poem. Follow one image through the whole text instead of listing five
    - Look for a **turn**: many poems change direction, often in the last stanza or the last line

## Useful phrases

| Purpose | Phrases |
|---|---|
| Introduction | <ul><li>The extract from …'s novel "…" (published in …) shows …</li><li>The short story "…" by … deals with …</li></ul> |
| Perspective | <ul><li>The story is told from the perspective of …</li><li>The first-person narrator gives the reader direct access to …</li><li>The narrator's limited insight makes the reader …</li></ul> |
| Characterisation | <ul><li>… is presented as …</li><li>This becomes apparent when she … (l. 23)</li><li>The way he addresses … reveals …</li><li>Her choice of words suggests …</li></ul> |
| Setting | <ul><li>The setting mirrors …</li><li>The setting contrasts sharply with …</li><li>The confined space underlines …</li></ul> |
| Structure | <ul><li>The extract opens in medias res, which …</li><li>The turning point occurs when … (l. 41)</li><li>The open ending leaves the reader with …</li></ul> |
| Language and imagery | <ul><li>The imagery of … runs through the whole text and suggests …</li><li>The repetition of "…" (ll. 5, 12, 30) emphasizes …</li><li>The shift from long to short sentences (l. 48 onwards) creates …</li></ul> |
| Theme | <ul><li>The text explores the question of …</li><li>Taken together, these elements point to …</li></ul> |
| Concluding | <ul><li>The analysis shows that …</li><li>All these elements work together to …</li></ul> |

## Checklist for analysing fictional texts

- [ ] Introduction with title, author, text type and the situation of the extract?
- [ ] Narrative perspective named **and** its effect explained?
- [ ] Every character trait backed by a quote with line reference?
- [ ] Setting connected to mood, character or theme, not just described?
- [ ] Imagery followed through the text instead of a device list?
- [ ] Simple present throughout?
- [ ] Conclusion that connects the observations to the theme?
- [ ] Only the aspects the [task](../language/operators.md) actually asks for?

## Common mistakes

- Retelling the story. If your text could be replaced by a plot summary, it is not an analysis
- Confusing speaker or narrator with the author
- Listing adjectives in a characterization with nothing to prove them
- Naming devices without effect: "The author uses a metaphor" and then nothing
- Writing about the setting as scenery, with no link to what the text is doing
- Treating a poem's form as an item to tick off, instead of asking what it does to the reading
- Mixing personal opinion into the analysis part. Keep evaluation where the task puts it
