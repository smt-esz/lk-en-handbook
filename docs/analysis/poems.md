# How to analyse a poem

A poem says something in a way no other text type could, and analysis means showing how the *how* produces the *what*. Poems turn up in class, in exams and occasionally in the final examination as material for a writing task.

!!! tip "At a glance"

    |   |   |
    |---|---|
    | **Purpose** | Show how form, sound and image work together to produce meaning |
    | **Structure** | Introduction → content and speaker → form and structure → language and imagery → sound → conclusion |
    | **Register & voice** | Formal, neutral perspective, **simple present** |
    | **Length** | As set by the task |
    | **Watch out** | **Never say "the poet" when you mean the speaker.** They are not the same person, and mixing them is the classic error |

**Operators for this task type:** a poem rarely turns up on the actual exam, so treat this as the pattern to expect from class rather than a well-worn exam formula.

| Operator | Example task |
|---|---|
| **interpret** (AFB III) | *Write an article for the Poetry Corner in which you interpret the poem "The Armful" by Robert Frost, also considering the effect it has on you.* (Abitur 2018) |
| **describe**, **analyse**, **examine**, **compare** | Standard operators for any literary analysis, see [fictional texts](fictional-texts.md) |

See [Operators](../language/operators.md).

## The speaker is not the poet

The voice in a poem is a construction, like a narrator in a novel. Robert Frost wrote the poem, the speaker is the person picking up parcels in it. Write *the speaker*, and keep *the poet* for choices made about the poem: the form, the line breaks, the title.

## Step by step

1. **Read it aloud, twice.** Poems are built for the ear. Where you stumble, something is happening.
2. **Paraphrase it in two sentences.** If you cannot, you do not yet know what it says.
3. **Ask who speaks, to whom, in what situation.** A poem is an utterance by someone in a position.
4. **Map the form.** Stanzas, line lengths, rhyme scheme, metre. Write the rhyme scheme in the margin as letters.
5. **Look for the break.** Almost every poem has a turn, a place where the argument, mood or image shifts. In a sonnet it usually sits at line 9. Find yours.
6. **Collect the imagery.** Which fields do the images come from: nature, war, machinery, the body? Recurring fields carry the theme.
7. **Listen for sound.** Alliteration, assonance, harsh or soft consonants, long or short vowels.
8. **Connect form to meaning.** This is the whole exercise. A regular metre that breaks down where the speaker loses control is worth three paragraphs of imagery spotting.
9. **Write with evidence.** Quotation plus line number for every claim, see [Quoting and citing](../language/mla-style-guide.md) for how to format it.

## What to look at

| Level | Questions | Terms you may need |
|---|---|---|
| **Speaker and situation** | Who speaks? To whom? Where, when, why? | lyrical speaker, addressee, occasion |
| **Content** | What happens or is described? Where does it turn? | volta, turn |
| **Structure** | Stanzas, line lengths, symmetry, progression | stanza, quatrain, couplet, sonnet, free verse |
| **Rhyme** | Scheme, and where it breaks | end rhyme, internal rhyme, half rhyme, couplet |
| **Rhythm** | Regular or irregular? Where does it change? | metre, iambic, stressed, unstressed |
| **Line and syntax** | Do sentences end with lines or run over? | enjambment, end-stopped line, caesura |
| **Imagery** | Which fields? Which recur? | metaphor, simile, symbol, personification, imagery |
| **Sound** | Which sounds cluster where? | alliteration, assonance, onomatopoeia |
| **Tone** | Bitter, tender, ironic, resigned? Does it shift? | tone, mood, irony |
| **Title** | What does it promise, and does the poem keep it? | |

## Form and meaning: the move that earns marks

Naming a device earns nothing. Connecting it to meaning earns everything.

| Observation alone | The same observation, connected |
|---|---|
| The poem uses enjambment in lines 7 to 8. | The sentence spills across the line break in ll. 7–8, so that the reader is pulled forward at exactly the moment the speaker says he cannot stop, and the form enacts the loss of control the words describe. |
| There is alliteration in line 3. | The clustered plosives of "bottles, buns" (l. 3) make the line physically awkward to say, which mirrors the awkward armful the speaker is trying to hold. |
| The rhyme scheme is abab. | The regular abab scheme holds the first two stanzas together, which makes its collapse into an unrhymed final line (l. 12) land as a small breakdown rather than a formal choice. |

## Useful phrases

| Purpose | Phrases |
|---|---|
| Introduction | <ul><li>The poem "…" by …, published in …, deals with …</li><li>In her poem "…", … explores the question of …</li></ul> |
| Speaker and situation | <ul><li>The speaker addresses …</li><li>The lyrical speaker looks back on …</li><li>The poem is spoken from the position of someone who …</li></ul> |
| Structure | <ul><li>The poem consists of three quatrains and a closing couplet.</li><li>The turn comes in l. 9, where the argument shifts from … to …</li><li>The final stanza returns to the image of …, but now …</li></ul> |
| Rhyme and rhythm | <ul><li>The regular abab rhyme scheme creates …</li><li>The metre breaks down in l. 11, which …</li><li>The rhyme fails exactly where the speaker …</li></ul> |
| Line and syntax | <ul><li>The enjambment in ll. 4–5 carries the reader across …</li><li>The caesura in l. 8 halts the line and forces …</li><li>The end-stopped lines of the first stanza give the poem a …</li></ul> |
| Imagery | <ul><li>The imagery is drawn from the field of …</li><li>The metaphor of … runs through the whole poem and suggests …</li><li>The image of … returns in l. 14, changed: now it …</li></ul> |
| Sound | <ul><li>The alliteration of "…" (l. 2) makes the line …</li><li>The long vowels in ll. 6–7 slow the poem down at the point where …</li></ul> |
| Tone | <ul><li>The tone is …, and it shifts in l. 9 towards …</li><li>What sounds affectionate at first turns out to be …</li></ul> |
| Concluding | <ul><li>Form and content work together here: …</li><li>The poem does not state its theme, it performs it.</li></ul> |

## Checklist for analysing a poem

- [ ] Have I paraphrased the poem for myself in two sentences?
- [ ] Have I written *the speaker* and not *the poet* for the voice?
- [ ] Have I found and named the turn?
- [ ] Have I mapped the rhyme scheme, and noted where it breaks?
- [ ] Have I connected at least three formal features to meaning?
- [ ] Does every claim carry a quotation and a line number?
- [ ] Have I looked at the title?
- [ ] Simple present throughout?
- [ ] Have I said what the poem is doing, not only what it contains?

## Common mistakes

- Calling the speaker "the poet" throughout
- Listing devices without effect: "The poem contains a metaphor, alliteration and enjambment"
- Paraphrasing stanza by stanza and calling it an analysis
- Reading biography into the poem when the text does not support it
- Ignoring the form entirely and treating the poem as a short prose text
- Missing the turn, which usually means missing the point
