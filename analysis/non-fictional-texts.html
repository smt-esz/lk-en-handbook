    How to analyse non-fictional texts

    At a glance
    Purpose Show how an author builds an argument and works on the reader
    Structure Introduction (title, author, source, date, text type, topic, thesis) → structure, argumentation, language → conclusion
    Register & voice Formal, neutral perspective, simple present
    Length As set by the task
    Watch out Never just spot a device. Statement → evidence (quote + line) → effect, every time

    Operators for this task type: non-fiction is the most common Part A1 text. As with fiction, an outline task comes first and an analyse task follows, both worth remembering.

    Operator Example task
    outline — most common opening operator Outline Rutherford's observations on private DNA tests and the concerns he expresses. (Abitur 2022)
    analyse — appears in almost every non-fiction task Analyse how the author raises the readers' awareness of the implications of DNA testing. (Abitur 2022)
    examine (the same operator as analyse) Examine the way the journalist appeals to his readers. (Abitur 2016)
    explain Explain why the debate about Islamic schools "cuts to the heart of Britain's stated philosophy on multiculturalism." (Abitur 2008)
    assess Analyse and assess the way the author presents the subject of minority integration. (Abitur 2008)

    See Operators.

    Step by step

    1. Read the task, then the text. Mark the aspects the task asks about while reading; underline striking language.
    2. Write the introduction. In the outline sub-task, one factual sentence with title, author, source, date, text type and topic. In the analysis sub-task do not repeat that data: say what you will examine, tied to the task. For example: The following analysis examines how structure and word choice make readers doubt the promise of private DNA tests.

    3. Analyse the structure and argumentation.

      • How is the text built? Where does the author state the thesis?
      • What kinds of support are used: facts, statistics, expert opinions, examples, anecdotes?
      • Does the author argue logically, emotionally, or both? Are counterarguments addressed?

    4. Analyse the language.

      • Register and tone: formal or colloquial, neutral or biased, serious or ironic? See Register and tone.
      • Word choice: emotionally charged words, evaluative adjectives, technical terms, personal pronouns?
      • Stylistic devices: identify, quote with line reference, and explain the effect in context.
      • Syntax: short punchy sentences, long complex ones, questions, imperatives?

    5. Connect language to intention. Every observation should answer: what does this do to the reader, and how does it serve the author's purpose?

    6. Conclude. Sum up how structure and language work together to achieve the author's intention. If the task includes evaluation: how convincing is the text, and why?

    Useful phrases

    Purpose Phrases
    Introduction
    • In the article "…" (published in …, date), … argues that …
    • The text, written by … for …, addresses the question of …
    Locating evidence
    • In line 12, …
    • At the beginning of the second paragraph, …
    • The phrase "…" (l. 34) …
    • Throughout the text, …
    Analysing effect
    • By using …, the author creates …
    • The metaphor of … conveys …
    • This emphasizes …
    • This makes the reader feel …
    Intention
    • The author aims to convince the reader that …
    • … sets out to alert the reader to …
    • This serves to …
    • The purpose behind this is …
    Concluding
    • On the whole, the author's strategy is to …
    • The analysis shows that …
    • Taken together, these means …

    Checklist for analysing non-fictional texts

    • Key data given once, and an introduction that says what the analysis will examine?
    • Every claim backed by a quote with line reference?
    • Effect explained for every device named?
    • Analysis linked to the author's intention?
    • Simple present throughout?

    Common mistakes

    • Retelling the content instead of analysing how it is presented
    • Device spotting: naming metaphors and anaphoras without explaining their effect
    • Quotes without line references, or line references without quotes
    • Analysing everything instead of what the task asks for
