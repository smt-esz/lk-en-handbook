    How to give a presentation

    At a glance
    Purpose Make an audience understand and remember something they did not know
    Structure Greeting → hook → outline of your points → main part → conclusion → questions
    Register & voice Neutral to formal. First person, addressing the audience directly. Simple present
    Length As set. Roughly 130 words per minute, so ten minutes is about 1300 words spoken
    Watch out Never read full sentences from your notes. Keywords only, eyes up

    Operators that show up in this task type:

    Operator Example task
    present Present the results of your survey to the class.
    illustrate Illustrate your point with examples from your research.

    present is the operator this task type is named after, it is almost always the one used. See Operators.

    Step by step

    1. Fix your one message first. Before you research anything, write down in one sentence what the audience should know or think afterwards. Everything that does not serve that sentence gets cut later, so decide now.
    2. Research from more than one source. Compare what they say. Where they disagree, you have found something worth presenting.
    3. Note where everything came from. You will need it for your source slide, and taking someone else's work without saying so is not a small thing.
    4. Choose three points, no more. An audience listening in a foreign language will hold on to three things. Five points means they remember none of them.
    5. Build the skeleton. Hook, outline, three points with evidence, conclusion that answers the hook.
    6. Write the hook last but rehearse it first. A question, a surprising figure, a thirty-second story. Never "Today I am going to talk about …".
    7. Make the visuals carry pictures, not text. A slide is a poster behind you, not a script in front of you. If your audience is reading, they are not listening.
    8. Reduce your notes to keywords. One index card, six keywords. If you can present from that, you know your material.
    9. Rehearse out loud, timed, standing up. Silent rehearsal at your desk tells you nothing about pace or breathing.
    10. Prepare for questions. Two or three you expect, and a way of saying you do not know that does not collapse the whole talk.

    Structure in detail

    Stage What happens Roughly
    Greeting Name yourself and your topic 15 seconds
    Hook Question, figure, image or short story 30 seconds
    Outline Tell them your three points in advance 20 seconds
    Main part Point, evidence, why it matters. Three times 70 % of your time
    Conclusion Return to the hook, state the one message 45 seconds
    Questions Invite them, answer briefly as available

    Useful phrases

    Purpose Phrases
    Opening
    • Good morning everyone. My name is … and today I would like to talk about …
    • Let me start with a number that surprised me: …
    • Have you ever wondered why … ?
    Giving an outline
    • I have divided my talk into three parts.
    • First I will …, then I will …, and finally I will …
    • Feel free to interrupt me if anything is unclear.
    Moving between points
    • That brings me to my second point.
    • Having looked at …, let us turn to …
    • So much for … . Now, …
    Referring to a visual
    • As you can see on this slide, …
    • This graph shows …
    • Let me draw your attention to the figure on the right.
    • The numbers speak for themselves: …
    Emphasizing
    • What matters most here is …
    • If you take away one thing today, let it be this: …
    • And this is the surprising part: …
    Explaining a term
    • By … I mean …
    • For those who have not come across the term, …
    • Put simply, …
    Involving the audience
    • Put yourself in their position for a moment.
    • How many of you have ever … ?
    • We all know the feeling.
    Concluding
    • To sum up, …
    • Let me come back to the question I started with.
    • Thank you for listening. I am happy to take your questions.
    Handling questions
    • That is a good question. As far as I know, …
    • I am not sure about that, but what I can say is …
    • Could you say a little more about what you mean?

    Delivery

    Delivery is marked, not decoration. The examination criteria for speaking are content, interaction and language, and delivery is how the first two reach anyone at all.

    • Volume and pace. Slower than feels natural. Nerves speed everyone up.
    • Eye contact. Pick three people in different parts of the room and rotate.
    • Pauses. After a key sentence, and before you move to a new point. Silence gives the audience a moment to catch up.
    • Your voice. Emphasis on the word that carries the meaning, not on every third word.
    • Hands. Somewhere visible and still, or gesturing on purpose. Not in your pockets and not on your phone.
    • Stand where they can see you, not in front of the projection.

    In a group presentation

    Everyone must carry a real share. A person who introduces the group and then falls silent has not presented. Divide by content, not by slide count, and hand over explicitly: "Lisa will now take you through the second part."

    Checklist for a presentation

    • Can I say my one message in a single sentence?
    • Three points, not five?
    • Does the hook survive being said out loud?
    • Do my slides show images and figures rather than sentences?
    • Are my notes keywords, not full sentences?
    • Have I timed a full run-through, standing and out loud?
    • Have I explained every technical term I use?
    • Are my sources named on a final slide?
    • Do I know what to say if I do not know an answer?
    • In a group: does every member have a real share?

    Common mistakes

    • Reading the slides aloud. The audience can read faster than you can speak, and they will
    • Five or six points, all of them thin, instead of three developed ones
    • A hook that is really an announcement: "Today I am going to talk about …"
    • Technical terms left unexplained because they were clear in the source
    • Speaking to the screen instead of to the room
    • Running over time, which almost always means the conclusion gets rushed and the one message never lands
